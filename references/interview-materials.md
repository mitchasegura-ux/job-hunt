# Interview materials

## Identify the interviewer first

This decides everything else, and getting it wrong wastes the whole exercise.

**Outsourced recruiter or HR screen.** Typically 30 minutes, and the questions
are: background, why this role, why leaving, requisition verification, salary,
timeline. They cannot evaluate technical depth and will not ask for it. If the
mock asks this person to define a VLAN, it is rehearsing a conversation that
will not happen.

**Hiring manager or small-company owner.** Technical questions are in scope and
they can judge the answers. At a small company there is no HR buffer, they may
decide quickly, and they care about whether the candidate can actually do the
work rather than whether the resume is accurate.

Clues: the inviting email's domain and signature, the company's size, whether an
ATS or a person sent it. An agency domain means a screen.

## Artifact 1: the mock interviewer prompt

A file the candidate pastes into a voice-mode assistant. It has to be strict,
because these default to flattery.

Include, explicitly:

- **Score every answer**, X/10, no exceptions. A first version that scored 4 of
  20 answers was useless for calibration.
- **Interrupt at 90 seconds** mid-sentence, in character, then dock the score.
  Rambling is the top failure mode and feedback only works if felt live.
- **Stay in character**, with banned question types listed by name if the
  interviewer is non-technical.
- **Cap the question count** to what fits the real slot. About 12 for 30 minutes.
- **End with a verdict**, pass or no pass, as the first line of the debrief.
- **Anchor the scoring scale** to a concrete example of a bad answer, so the
  model has a reference point instead of grading on vibes.

Also include the candidate's known weak points from any previous run, quoted,
with instructions to score a repeat offense lower and say it is a repeat.

## Artifact 2: the printed reference card

Two pages the candidate keeps in front of them during the call. Design for
reading mid-sentence under pressure, not for study.

- **A scripted opening line per question**, then short clauses. Starting is the
  hard part; once moving they know the material. Full sentences throughout make
  people sound read-aloud, bare keywords leave the high-stakes moments to
  improvisation.
- **A DO NOT box at the top**, three items, the things that lose the interview.
- **The salary block scripted verbatim** and timed under 20 seconds.
- **A story bank**: three stories, one line each, mapped to the questions each
  one answers. This is what lets a candidate reuse preparation across many
  questions instead of memorizing per-question answers.
- **Whitespace for notes**, because they will write on it.
- Bold all-caps section labels so they can find their place after looking away.

Keep it to two pages. Verify by rendering.

## Salary, which is per-role

The right move depends on where their floor sits relative to the posted range.

- **Published range comfortably above their current pay**: anchor at the upper
  middle with a reason drawn from the requisition, and never name a number below
  the published floor.
- **Published floor sitting on their hard limit**: the risk inverts. Now the
  danger is landing anywhere in the bottom half, so ask what the range depends
  on before naming anything.
- **A screening call is a filter, not a negotiation.** A range reads as informed
  where a single number reads as inflexible. The point number belongs at offer
  stage.
- Many states bar employers from requiring salary history. Prepare a warm
  deflection rather than a legal citation, which lands as adversarial.

The strongest framing names the experience gap first, then overcomes it on
breadth. Self-aware plus calibrated is what reads as serious.
